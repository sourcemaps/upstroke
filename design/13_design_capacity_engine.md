## 13. Capacity engine (P9)

The router's economics depend on which pool pays. Pools have different shapes, and the engine models them explicitly:

| Pool kind | Example | Unit | Reset shape |
|---|---|---|---|
| Subscription windows | Claude Max 5x/20x | tokens (est.) | 5-hour rolling + weekly cap |
| Metered credits | Copilot on AI-Credit billing (post-Jun 2026) | credits ≈ $ | monthly allowance + PAYG |
| Legacy request pools | Copilot annual plans | premium requests × per-model multiplier | monthly |
| API keys | Anthropic/OpenAI direct | dollars | none (budget only) |
| Local | home-server models via an OpenAI-compatible endpoint | unlimited | none (hardware-bound) |

**Estimation sources**, in trust order: (1) rate-limit signals from the CLIs — ground truth; a `RateLimited` outcome immediately marks the pool exhausted, demotes or parks frontier-hungry tasks, and sets a retry-at-reset timer; (2) self-metering of everything the engine spawned; (3) ccusage-style parsing of local agent logs, which captures the user's *interactive* sessions drawing from the same pool; (4) optionally, explicitly selected first-party read-only capacity endpoints, including known undocumented endpoints admitted by the owner — asked by the capacity tracker, outside the engine (below), treated as fragile and never load-bearing: a provider's report can lower an estimate and can never raise one. Estimates are always conservative: a per-pool `safety_margin` (usage on other machines is invisible to local log parsing) and a `reserve` floor that keeps headroom for the user's own interactive work.

**Rate limits and resets, as built (2026-11-14).** Source 1 is narrower than the paragraph above reads. Beside a fixed list of phrases (`5-hour limit reached`, `usage limit`, Copilot's credit wording, API errors), a failed attempt is `RateLimited` when its text says it hit a quota window's limit (`You've hit your 5-hour limit`; any number of hours, `session` or `weekly`), or the limit of one of Claude's four model names — Opus, Sonnet, Haiku, Fable — with a reset after it (`You've hit your Fable limit · resets Nov 14, 9am (UTC)`). A model name without a reset, or any other word (`context`, `token`, `request`), is an ordinary task failure. The reset recorded as `pool_exhausted.reset_at`, and carried into `capacity_snapshot`, is read only from the limit's own text: the `resets` that follows the recognised limit on its line, or, when nothing after the limit on its line is a reset, a `resets` that begins the very next line. A reset with no such limit before it — `MCP cache resets 9am (UTC)` alone, or on a later line — is never read, and when the limit's own reset cannot be read (a zone other than UTC, a malformed time) none is recorded and no other reset stands in. Stderr, when that is what signalled the limit, is read before the attempt's reason, and the first of the two that holds a recognised limit decides: the reason is read only when the stderr holds none. Only Claude Code's text is read, since no other vendor's reset format is known, so every other agent records none. The messages give no year, and the short form no date, so the reset is the occurrence nearest the moment the text is read: a limit read a second after its reset keeps that reset, not one a day ahead. A pool gets one `pool_exhausted` per distinct reset. A repeat refusal naming the same reset, or none, writes nothing; the fold, and so a replay or resume, reports the latest signal's reset; a rate-limited deferral with no signal of its own keeps the known reset; and a success retires the pool, reset and all, so the next refusal writes one again. Both engines now record `pool_exhausted` under the same rules. The schema-4 engine appends it before the rate-limited settlement, so a known reset is durable before it, and a stop between them leaves only the signal, which a resume reads; the legacy writer, which runs only when a run of schemas 1–3 resumes, still appends it after the settlement (`PR380-RESET-LOST-BETWEEN-SETTLEMENT-AND-SIGNAL`, deferred: reordering it there also needs the replay to keep a reviewer's signal past the settlement that retires its pool). A schema-4 run now waits out a known reset at most 5 h 15 min ahead, to two minutes past it, as one deferral (§19); further ahead, or unknown, the ladder still defers three times and then asks a human. A worker's agent with more than one pool may hold a reset already passed: the run waits only to two minutes past it, and after that there is no reset wait, only the ordinary back-off. One pool per agent, and a reviewer's limit, wait only for a reset ahead, as before failover. A reset is spent once its pool refuses again two minutes or more past it, and is neither waited for nor bound on. The retry-at-reset timer above is otherwise not built. On a schema-4 run, a worker's limit now fails over before it waits, when another pool of its agent is not held exhausted (§19).

**Machine-level, as built (2026-11-23).** Capacity is tracked for the machine, not for a repository. A subscription is the operator's: two repositories on one machine drain the same window, and a rate limit one of them met is a fact about the pool the other is about to use. So `upstroke capacity` and the `validate` and `--dry-run` preview fold **every run on the machine**, where they folded the latest run of the repository they were started in. Nothing is recorded twice to make that possible: each schema-4 run already keeps its private half under the machine directory (`~/.upstroke/runs/<run-id>/`, §15) with an `owner.json` naming its public directory, which is the index, and each run's `events.jsonl` stays the one record of what it did. A run of schemas 1–3 has no owner record and is found only from inside its own repository. Each log is read by the reader of its own schema: a schema-4 log by the observer `status` reads it with, a stable read of its committed lines and a checked replay, so a log the engine would refuse to resume contributes nothing; a log of schemas 1–3 by the sequential reader. A run is folded once, by its id (since 2026-11-24): a repository copied to a second place with its `.upstroke/` holds a second log of each of its runs, and logs of one id that hold the same events are one run seen twice, folded once with nothing said. Where they differ, the log folded is the one the run's owner record names, else the one holding more events, else the first by path, so the choice never turns on the repository the views were started in; a warning names the run and both logs. A log that cannot be read beside one that can is named the same way, and its run, being folded, is not counted among those that could not be read. The runs' capacity evidence — `pool_exhausted` signals, settled attempts, interrupted attempts, the review passes of a merge verification — is put in one sequence by event time, each run's own order kept exactly, and the rules of one run are applied to it, each refusal held as its own run's: a pool is exhausted while any run's refusal of it stands, showing the reset of the refusal applied last; a signal sets its run's refusal and its reset; a rate-limited settlement marks its pool for its run and keeps a known reset; and an attempt the pool served retires its own run's refusal of it, in that run's order, but another run's only when the attempt passed and its service is dated strictly after that refusal (since 2026-11-24). A run writes an attempt's record after its gates and its judgement, so the service of an attempt that passed is dated by the attempt's `attempt_started` plus its measured duration, never later than the record, and a reviewer's pool by the worker's end, the earliest the review can have run. A failed attempt is not dated, whatever its failure and whatever usage or cost it recorded: its record does not say when the pool served it, and a worker that never reached its provider still counts as served. It retires its own run's refusals alone, as does an attempt with no dated start, so the machine-level view errs toward exhausted until an attempt passes on the pool; and the pool is the unit, so an attempt that passed retires a refusal that named one model. A run of schemas 1–3 is dated the same way, its attempt known by task and attempt number. Event times are whole seconds, and evidence of one second from different runs is ordered by what it is and by run id, never by the repository the views were started in, so one machine gives one answer wherever it is asked: a refusal waits for another run's success of the same second on its pool, and no success is dated after a refusal of its own second, so that success leaves the pool exhausted rather than clearing it, while one run's own refusal and then success in one second still clear it, in that run's order. Where two runs' refusals and successes of one second cross, each refusing a pool before serving the one the other refused, both refusals stand. Inside one run a late settlement still retires that run's own newer refusal, and a failed attempt that run's refusal of the pool it ran on. Self-metered draw counts the current calendar month (UTC), the period `monthly_allowance` is measured against, so a pool's ceiling is its allowance less everything upstroke drew from it on this machine this month, where it was less what one run drew. `[capacity] scope = "repo"` (§17), or `upstroke capacity --scope repo`, folds this repository's runs alone. A run that cannot be read shows nothing: a warning names it, the scope line counts it — with none readable it says the runs found could not be read, never that there are none — and the rest are folded. That is a run none of whose logs either reader accepts, or whose owner record exists and leads to no log, because it cannot be read or parsed, names another run, or names no run directory. A private half with no owner record is a run of schemas 1–3 and is not counted, and neither is the record of a run the repository itself lists, which is read from there. A live run still binds and fails over on its own signals alone (§19): the machine-level fold feeds the read-only views, and making it an input to a run's binding is capacity-driven routing, which is not built. What no fold on this machine can see is unchanged: other machines and the operator's interactive sessions, which is what the margin and the reserve are for.

**The capacity tracker, as built (2026-11-25).** Source 4 needs HTTP, and the engine never speaks it (§4.2). So the asking is done by a separate component, the capacity tracker, whose billing operation is `upstroke capacity --refresh`: for each pool that names a `usage_provider` (§17) it asks that provider's usage endpoint what the account has drawn, and stores the answer under the machine directory, `~/.upstroke/capacity/usage/` (§15). It dispatches no agent, runs no gate, owns no git and writes no event log, and nothing in `run` or `resume` calls it or waits for it; they read the stored reports as files. The direction is the point: a run's behaviour is a function of its event log and the files on disk, and a network answer that arrived or did not arrive mid-run is neither. The split is held mechanically, by the means every other effect boundary here is: the tracker's entry points are on the effect denylist, so a module that states no allowance cannot name them, and a census over every production source file fails if anything but the CLI does.

The request is made by `curl`, as a subprocess, through the Runner — the way upstroke reaches everything else outside itself. The binary links no HTTP client and no TLS stack, and the operator's proxy and certificate settings are `curl`'s to honour. `curl` is given only `https://` addresses fixed in the binary, follows no redirect, reads no `.curlrc`, and is told a time and a size limit; the address and the headers reach it on standard input, so the credential appears in no argument, no environment variable the tracker sets, and no error. An absent `curl` fails the refresh of the pools that needed it, with the lookup's own account, and nothing else.

**Which endpoints.** Explicitly selected first-party HTTPS GET endpoints, with credentials the provider authorizes for this integration. The owner may admit known undocumented read-only capacity endpoints (§20); that admission does not authorize reuse of credentials restricted to native applications. The documented billing readers remain `anthropic-cost` (the Admin API's cost report), `openai-cost` (organization costs), `github-ai-credits` and `github-premium-requests` (GitHub's per-account Copilot billing). The first admitted undocumented reader is Claude reset metadata: exactly `https://api.anthropic.com/api/oauth/usage?cedar_ember=1&skip_spend=1`, with `anthropic-beta: oauth-2025-04-20`. No URL input, redirect, POST, redemption or account-changing operation is admitted. Codex stays on its official app-server `account/rateLimits/read` surface, not a direct ChatGPT HTTP endpoint. Claude's statusline `rate_limits` and the Copilot SDK `account.getQuota` remain possible subscription-window sources through the vendors' own tools; those readers are not built.

**Voluntary reset credits, as built (2026-11-25).** `upstroke capacity --reset-credits claude` reads an account's voluntary reset inventory independently of configured billing pools. These grants are distinct from the automatic weekly or session reset clock: their counts are not capacity, and their use-by dates are not quota reset times. The result is advisory, is not stored, never enters pool arithmetic and never clears an exhaustion signal. The reader is a typed tracker operation for the future handler to reuse; the handler itself is not implemented. Ordinary capacity/report, refresh, run and resume do not call this reader.

Only `UPSTROKE_CLAUDE_RESET_TOKEN`, supplied by the operator, supplies the bearer. It must be explicitly provider-authorized for this integration; ordinary native-app-only subscription credentials are unsuitable without that authorization. The reader never discovers or extracts Claude Code credentials, collects or refreshes a CLI login, or impersonates a vendor CLI. A missing or invalid credential yields an actionable unavailable result with zero requests. The bounded Runner-backed curl GET sends the secret on stdin. Diagnostics contain fixed categories rather than raw responses, process errors or credentials.

The independently implemented Rust reader interprets `cedar_ember.eligible` and `cedar_ember.grants`. Each grant reports its nonnegative integer `resets_left`, parsed `ends_at` use-by timestamp, a fixed kind vocabulary (`weekly`, `session`, `full`, `fable`, otherwise unknown), and optional `gives_back_weekly`, `paused` and `usable_now` booleans. `gives_back_weekly: true` selects `fable`; an absent type permits inference from recognized `clears` windows. It prints no arbitrary provider strings or identifiers. Missing, null, malformed or unrecognized fields stay unknown; an explicit empty grant array is known empty, while a missing or invalid inventory and failed statuses are unavailable. Availability and expiry are separate: expired, paused, exhausted or explicitly unusable grants are never usable, nor are grants of an explicitly ineligible account. Positive usability requires affirmative eligibility, a positive count, a known future expiry, `paused: false` and `usable_now: true`; incomplete evidence remains unknown unless a known disqualifier already applies. Reads are bounded to 1 MiB and 128 grants. Wire facts were supplied from the MIT-licensed `pingdotgg/t3code@226113fee0682579155702b9e182c5a893aabbcf`, `apps/server/src/provider/claudeResetCredits.ts`; no upstream implementation is copied.

All four report **spend for the current calendar month**, which is what a billing endpoint knows; none reports what is left. The pool's `monthly_allowance`, in the same units, supplies the other half, and with `"auto"` the report is shown as a note and bounds nothing. A response is untrusted input: each parser reads the fields it needs, and refuses rather than guesses on a currency or a unit it does not expect, a negative or non-finite number, or a page that says more pages follow — each of which would otherwise become a spend figure that is too low, and so a ceiling that is too high. The credential is read from the environment variable the pool names in `usage_token_env`; the tracker reads no agent CLI's credential store, and no credential is ever written to a file. One pool's failure is that pool's line in the output and never ends the refresh, and a failed refresh leaves the report stored before in place.

**What a stored report is worth.** A report is a point-in-time reading, and what makes an old one still worth holding is that usage inside one window only grows: a month with 60 of 100 drawn at noon has at least 60 drawn at one, so "at most 40% left" stays true for as long as the window does not reset. That is the validity rule, and it is why a report's age alone never retires it. A window whose reset has passed says nothing; a window that names no reset is believed for fifteen minutes; a report dated ahead of this machine's clock, or from a provider the pool no longer names, says nothing at all. **A report is bound to the source it was fetched with** (the account since 2026-11-23, the credential's variable since 2026-11-24): usage only grows within one account's window, and a provider that reports per key answers for whichever organization its key belongs to. So every report records the name of the environment variable its credential was read from, which is the pool's `usage_token_env` at the refresh, and, where the provider reports per account, the account it read, which is the pool's `usage_account`. It records names and never a credential, and a provider that reports per key records no account. A report says nothing unless its provider, its variable and, where the provider reads one, its account are each the one the pool names now. A part the report does not record matches nothing, and neither does a part the pool does not name: a report stored before a part was recorded cannot be told apart from another source's, and a pool that names no `usage_token_env`, or no `usage_account` its provider needs, is never refreshed, so no report is about it. A note says which part differs, or that the report predates recording it, and that `upstroke capacity --refresh` replaces the report with one for the source the pool names; for a pool that names no such key, it says the refresh will not run until the pool names one. The variable is matched by its name, so a variable that comes to hold another organization's key under the same name is not detected. Where several windows stand, the tightest binds. In the estimate a report ranks below self-metering, as the trust order above has it, and the rule that follows from "never load-bearing" is that **a provider report only ever sets or lowers a ceiling**: it never raises one a higher-ranked source set, never replaces a rate-limit signal, says nothing about an unmetered pool, and — whatever it reports — never says *exhausted*, which stays a signal's word alone. An estimate it set reads `[provider-reported]`, after the pool's margin and reserve like any other.

**Discovery — `upstroke connect`.** Pools are connected, not configured: `connect` scans PATH for official CLIs, checks auth state, detects each plan's quota shape, enumerates available models, and writes the user-level pools file. Tier classification comes from a **capability catalog** shipped with the binary (static data — the no-HTTP invariant holds), with a pragmatic prior for unknowns: providers price their own models, so per-model multipliers and per-token rates rank capability. A model absent from the catalog is never auto-selected — pin it or update. Decision logs later calibrate the catalog with measured pass rates per tier and task kind.

**Credential profiles.** One vendor can back several pools — two Claude Max accounts, say — told apart by each pool's `profile` (§17). A profile is the absolute path of the directory handed to the agent's CLI as its credential location, through the provider's own profile mechanism: an environment variable on the subprocess rather than a token the engine ever handles (invariants 2 and 5). An attempt on a schema-4 run binds the first pool of its agent, in file order, that the run's signals do not hold exhausted. With every one held, it binds the first whose recorded reset is two minutes past, or else the first; a retry that resumes its session keeps that session's pool. `connect` does not enumerate profiles, because no vendor lists them: the operator writes `profile`, and `connect` carries it across a rewrite. A worker runs under the profile of the pool its attempt binds, which the host runner sets as `CLAUDE_CONFIG_DIR`; review and probe processes still use the agent's first pool, whatever is held. A worker's rate limit fails over to another pool of its agent that is not held exhausted (§19). A profile is supported for Claude Code on the host runner only. A profile the run cannot honour, such as a relative path or a missing directory, or one that a set `CLAUDE_CODE_OAUTH_TOKEN` would override, is refused before any agent CLI is started: by a fresh run in `prepare_fresh`, before its lease, and by a resume in its pre-flight, under its locks (§17). Pre-flight then runs the CLI's capability probe under the profile and, for Claude Code alone, its sign-in check (`claude --setting-sources "" auth status --json`), on a fresh run and on a resume, once under each account Claude Code's pools use, in file order: each profile they name, and the default account when any of them names none or Claude Code has no pool. It stops at the first account that is signed out or whose check fails to answer, and that account refuses the run before it starts or resumes; a CLI that cannot say passes. The check asks with no user, project or local settings loaded, as a worker loads none, so a credential that only a settings file supplies (an `apiKeyHelper`, say) does not count. An agent whose pools use more than sixteen distinct accounts is refused before its sign-in is checked. Other agents' sign-in is not checked at pre-flight, so a signed-out Codex fails at its first attempt (`PR408-CODEX-SIGN-IN-NOT-PREFLIGHTED`). Selecting between several same-kind pools is v0.2 capacity work, and it changes three things: estimates must **attribute** usage per profile rather than aggregate it (local-log parsing reads per-account state, so summing reports one healthy pool where there is one exhausted and one fresh); `reserve` becomes asymmetric, since a plan bought for unattended work has no interactive use to protect; and independent reset windows turn a rate limit from *wait* into *rebind and continue* (§10.5) — the single biggest practical gain for an overnight run. Affinity will still govern the order: prompt caches are per-account, so the binder is to drain one pool toward its reserve and then switch, rather than round-robining and paying cache-cold on every task. Of all this, only failover between pools of the same agent on the same model is built (r6, question 7): the order is file order, no estimate enters the choice, and per-profile attribution, the asymmetric reserve and affinity are not built. **Decided 2026-11-25, not built:** a session upstroke runs, bound to a pool other than the one the capacity handler (below) would choose, is moved to that pool between turns, never within one: its transcript is carried into the other pool's profile and the session resumed there through the CLI's own resume, recorded as one event in the run's log, and a session is not moved back and forth between pools whose standing is close, since every move pays the cold cache that affinity avoids. A session a person started outside upstroke is placed by a launcher when it starts; one already running on another pool's account is reported, and moved only when the person asks, or by the handler if a machine setting, off by default, allows it. Claude Code comes first, as the one agent with profiles.

**Subscription usage, as built (2026-11-26).** `capacity --subscription-usage claude
[--auth cli|bearer]` is a pool-independent advisory report. The default `cli` route reads
windows only, never grant counts or grant status, and asks the
signed-in official CLI exactly once, with no shell:
`claude -p --input-format stream-json --output-format stream-json --verbose
--no-session-persistence --strict-mcp-config --tools "" --setting-sources ""`.
Stdin is one newline-terminated JSON control request followed by EOF:
`{"type":"control_request","request_id":"usage-only","request":{"subtype":"get_usage","skip_behaviors":true}}`.
There is no prompt, model turn, reset/claim or interaction. The existing Runner probe
bounds the process to 45 seconds and bounds capture; the reader also refuses stdout or
stderr over 1 MiB. Timeout, output-limit and exit status are checked before parsing.
Only one successful `control_response` matching `usage-only` supplies a payload under
`response.response`. Proper unrelated stream events are ignored; malformed, incomplete,
duplicate or unsuccessful matching responses fail with fixed diagnostics and no raw output.

The CLI retains its own login and inherited `CLAUDE_CONFIG_DIR` (or its default).
For example, `CLAUDE_CONFIG_DIR=/absolute/profile upstroke capacity --subscription-usage claude`
selects an already signed-in profile. Upstroke does not inspect, export or refresh CLI
credentials; the CLI owns normal authentication. The child receives an empty
`UPSTROKE_CLAUDE_RESET_TOKEN` override. Windows alone never resolve this token or send HTTP.
Adding `--reset-credits claude` independently calls the existing reset reader once, using
that operator-supplied, explicitly provider-authorized bearer. The two reports are composed
even if either source fails: windows survive missing/refused credentials or a failed GET,
and grants survive a CLI failure. No grants are inferred from native CLI output.

`--auth bearer` instead selects an all-bearer report: windows and grants from the existing
single fixed reset GET and transport, with no CLI call. Both data flags with `--auth bearer`
use this same one-read report, never a second grant GET. Missing or invalid credentials
produce unavailable without any external call. Neither flow falls back or retries for
missing readings. No CLI user-agent is added to HTTP, and this bearer is never tried on
another endpoint. Endpoint admission does not authorize native token export.

The native payload's `rate_limits_available` controls window reporting; false means disabled
and missing or malformed means unavailable. `rate_limits` contains independent `five_hour`,
`seven_day` and optional `model_scoped[]`; any native `cedar_ember` is ignored. Percentages are finite
numbers from 0 through 100; timestamps use the existing RFC3339 validator and UTC renderer.
Missing or malformed fields stay unknown. Model labels use a fixed Sonnet/Opus/Haiku/Fable
allowlist (including their Claude-prefixed and lowercase spellings); other names print
`unknown model`. At most 128 model entries are read; an oversized group is unreported
without suppressing ordinary windows or grants. Raw bearer responses may supply windows
beside `cedar_ember`; they are not guaranteed by the grant query. An absent `model_scoped`
permits raw `limits[]` with `kind: weekly_scoped`, `scope.model.display_name`, `percent`
and `resets_at`.

An actual `cedar_ember` object in the bearer response uses the same validated grant parser and renderer as
`--reset-credits`. Null/missing grants never become zero, an empty array stays known empty,
and window availability never gates grant parsing or vice versa. Automatic window resets
and utilization percentages are labelled separately from voluntary counts and use-by times.
Nothing is persisted, redeemed or fed into pool arithmetic; ordinary capacity, refresh, run
and resume do not invoke this report. Results exit 0 even when unavailable. The owner
observed the native invocation online on Claude Code 2.1.332 with both ordinary windows
and model windows present and `cedar_ember: null`. Implementation verification uses offline
synthetic fixtures for both routes; no live bearer read or authorization to export native
credentials is claimed.

**Strategies** (`routing.strategy.mode`):

- `conserve` — classic cost minimization: route down aggressively, escalate only on failure, defer frontier-hungry tasks toward window resets when a pool is projected to run dry.
- `value-max` — subscription yield management: prepaid capacity that would expire unused has zero marginal cost, so surplus near a reset biases default tiers **up** (spend-down mode) — Opus for implementation, frontier review everywhere — subject to `min`/`max` bounds and the reserve floor. *No shipped tool does this (verified Aug 2026); it is the headline.*
- `deadline` — wall-clock first: maximize parallel throughput within capacity, spilling to API dollars when justified by a configured $/hour ceiling.

The ledger accounts every attempt in both currencies: API-equivalent dollars (honestly labeled — subscription spend is notional) and pool units drained. Where a worker's route reports no spend at all — Copilot's does not (§16) — the total it contributes to is marked `?` rather than presented as complete, since a cross-vendor review makes that the normal case rather than a corner of it. Budgets exist per run ($), per task ($), and per pool (fraction).

**Sequencing:** v0.1 ships the capacity engine **read-only** — the dry-run preview and `upstroke capacity` show each pool's estimated remaining capacity, resets, and what each strategy *would* do. v0.2 wires it into live routing. This de-risks estimator fragility before any routing depends on it, and the preview alone is the demo that sells the product. As built, `upstroke capacity` and `validate` fold every run in scope, whatever its schema — every run on the machine, or this repository's alone (above): rate-limit signals are held by the same rule a resumed run uses, applied across the runs in the order their events happened, and the self-metered spend is every settled attempt's, every interrupted attempt's (unpriced, on the pool it started on) and every integration review pass's, over the current calendar month. A log that cannot be read shows nothing from that run, and the report says so: a warning names the run and the scope line counts it. The machine-level fold and the capacity tracker feed those same read-only views: they change what is estimated and from how much evidence, and nothing binds on either yet. How live routing is wired in was decided on 2026-11-25: through the capacity handler below.

**The capacity handler, decided 2026-11-25 (not built).** Capacity becomes a resource a run requests rather than one it discovers by failing: the engine asks for capacity for an action, and a capacity handler outside the engine grants it, makes it wait or denies it, arbitrating across every run on the machine. Its grant is the capacity permit; no second permit is kept inside a run. It comes in stages: first it grants pools and waits on resets, as a run's own signals do today (§19), and the operator's priorities between runs come after, in a file the handler owns under `~/.upstroke/capacity/` rather than in `upstroke.toml`, where a repository's own file is laid over the machine's (§17). It chooses a pool, and so a profile; the agent and model stay the binder's to choose per attempt (§10.5), and a plan amendment's rebind (§5) is needed only to change what the plan fixed for a task: its pin, its tier or rung, or its effort. The run records each grant in its event log (§15): a replay re-reads the grants recorded and asks the handler nothing, and a resume asks for the actions it goes on to take, as a fresh run does. The handler is `upstroke capacity serve` (§18), started by the operator, and the protocol a run speaks to it is public and versioned, for other programs to implement. With no handler configured, a run behaves as today: this section's own policy when a pools file exists, and every action granted with no pool otherwise. When a configured handler cannot be reached, a run falls back to that same policy; a machine may choose to wait instead, a choice kept in the handler's own file, which is also what marks a handler as configured. The handler will read subscription windows through the vendors' own CLIs and may reuse the tracker's admitted read-only capacity readers described in **Which endpoints** above. Claude reset availability and use-by dates will be advisory inputs available to it through the implemented reset reader, distinct from automatic window clocks and from the capacity permits it grants to runs. Neither redemption nor handler integration is built. `PR5-CAPACITY-NOT-A-TOPOLOGY-RESOURCE` (`findings/`) closes when the priority stage lands.

**Banked-grant planning, owner decision 2026-11-26 (future design, not built).** The separate machine-wide capacity planner decides when to use banked grants across runs; runs request capacity. It estimates the account's rate of use per window from successive readings within that same window, splitting history at refills and plan changes, and considers queued priorities and overlapping weekly, model and short limits. It compares time to exhaustion with natural refill and grant expiry, then compares redeeming now, deferring until useful, and letting the grant expire. These comparisons are future scheduling policy, not observed or live redemption behavior: value only useful work unlocked before the relevant expiry/refill and downstream effects of moving the refill schedule, never nominal restored quota alone. Grant expiry is the last redemption time, not a deadline for spending restored allowance; expiry loses the opportunity to restore capacity, not current quota.

Reset effects must be rules per provider, grant and window, not an unconditional provider-wide boolean. Anthropic's weekly reset day and time remain fixed. Session and weekly grants restore their named allowances; Fable restores the weekly usage spent on Fable and refills a five-hour allowance if the account has one. The fixed weekly clock does not establish five-hour scheduling. [Anthropic: What is a limit reset?](https://support.claude.com/en/articles/17007452-what-is-a-limit-reset)

OpenAI's banked resets cover plan-specific allowances: an eligible covered allowance below full is restored, while one already at 100 percent remaining is not restarted. A restarted window's new schedule replaces its old refill; the planner must check the reported next refresh rather than calculate it from the redemption click, since a projected timestamp can change before use starts. These are banked-grant rules; paid-reset clock rules are outside this policy. [OpenAI: How resets work in Codex](https://help.openai.com/en/articles/20001498-how-resets-work-in-codex)

Unredeemed grants never count as spendable quota. Missing inventory and unknown reset effects stay unknown. After an authorized redemption, the planner must re-read actual balances and timestamps before allocating restored capacity. No purchases are in scope.
